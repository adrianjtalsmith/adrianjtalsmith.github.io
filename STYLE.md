# Style primer

How the prose on this site should sound, drawn from a close reading of ten
single-authored pieces (2009–2026): the Elizans draft, *Unmistaken* (2024),
*Introspection and bodily awareness* (2024), *Bodily structure and body
representation* (2021), *Perspectival structure and agentive self-location*
(2017), the Open MIND commentary (2016), *Mental activity and the sense of
ownership* (2015), the Schwitzgebel review (2013), *What reason could there be
to believe in pre-reflective bodily self-consciousness?* (2012), and *Acting on
(bodily) experience* (2009). Roughly 84,000 words and 2,800 sentences.

The primer has two uses. It tells a human editor what to keep and what to cut.
And it can be handed to an assistant, with COPY.md, as the brief for any new
text on the site.

---

## 1. The sound, in a paragraph

A plain observation or a homely case comes first, and the abstraction is earned
from it. Positions are given names and the names then carry the argument.
Claims are stated in the first person without fuss, and the reasons for them
follow at once, often in a sentence beginning "For". Opponents are granted their
point before the turn, which almost always begins "But". The vocabulary is
exact and slightly old-fashioned (apposite, posit, construal, in so far as,
whilst), the humour is dry and parenthetical, and nothing is a slogan. The
reader is treated as a colleague who will follow a distinction if it is drawn
carefully and will not be impressed by a flourish.

## 2. Sentence level

### 2.1 Length and rhythm

| | |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 28–31 words in every paper |
| Median | 25–28 words |
| Sentences of 12 words or fewer | about 1 in 9 |
| Sentences of 40 words or more | about 1 in 5 |

The long sentences are built by accretion: a claim, then a qualifying clause,
then a parenthetical citation or aside, then a second clause that draws the
consequence. They do not sprawl, because each added clause does a job.

The short sentences are pivots, and they are where the voice is most audible.
They deny, redirect, or name:

- "But nothing about interacting in language requires this."
- "They meet to discuss."
- "Forks are in fact routine."
- "Call our film-watcher Johanna."
- "The underlying model is then updated."
- "But we know exactly what she addressed."

A paragraph typically has one such sentence, rarely more. Two short punchy
sentences in a row read as advertising and do not occur.

### 2.2 How sentences begin

"But" opens one sentence in twenty-five, and is the second most common opener
after "The". It is preferred to "However," by roughly seven to one. Other
habitual openers, in order of frequency: "Indeed,", "Moreover,", "Thus,",
"Accordingly,", "Rather,", "So,", "Still,", "Yet". Sentence-initial "And" is
used without embarrassment, about once per 1,500 words.

"For" as a reason-giving opener is a signature:

> These are not cases of illusion; they are cases of imaginative perception.
> **For** in each case the object I perceive appears to be how I imagine it to be.

> Whether or not interpretationism offers the right methodology for
> understanding human psychology, it is a natural enterprise and seems
> especially promising … **For** although interpretationism concerns the
> intelligibility of the ascription of attitudes, these principles reflect an
> overall requirement of rationality on the target.

### 2.3 Naming

Views, cases, and kinds of person are given labels early, and the label is then
used in place of the description: *the standard view* and *the minimal view*;
PST and AST; *Film*, *HMD*, *HMD\**; *Alternative Appearance*, *Misleading
Appearance*, *Perceptual Error*; *Elizans*, *the person frame*, *the Gilbert
principle*; *invariance*. The move is announced explicitly:

- "On what I will call the standard view …"
- "Call this assumption invariance."
- "Call the target of Elizan thought her interlocutor."
- "To employ a label that is neutral on the issues in question, call any case in
  which … a case of Alternative Appearance."

Characters are named too, and they hold views and argue: Sally, Johanna,
Birgitte, Rasmus. A named character can carry a whole section's exposition.

### 2.4 The first person

The first person is used freely and always for a specific purpose: to announce
what will be argued, to mark the scope of a claim, or to record a judgement.

- Announcing: "I will argue", "I argue", "My aim here is to", "My strategy will
  be", "My hope is that", "I propose".
- Scoping: "I take it that", "I surmise", "I admit", "I should note", "for
  present purposes", "for all that is said here", "I leave that as a task for
  others (or another occasion)".
- Judging: "I think", "I would resist", "I find attractive", "I suspect" (rare).

Two attitudes coexist. Confidence about the argument: "I will argue that this
latter claim is false and that the standard view ought to be abandoned."
Modesty about novelty: "I offer little that is new"; "philosophers of film have
seen far down the road that I wish were better travelled"; "This paper develops
ideas mentioned in passing … given how far departed the current treatment is."

### 2.5 Hedges

Hedges are frequent but precise. They fix the scope of a claim; they do not
soften it. The working set is *perhaps*, *plausibly* / *it is plausible that*,
*certainly*, *surely*, *at least*, *roughly*, *in some sense*, *in a sense*,
*it may be that*, *arguably*, *often*, *typically*. "Surely" and "certainly"
usually appear inside a concession that is about to be turned:

> Yes, she would have been deluded if she believed that she was talking to a
> human; but he gives us ample reason to think that his secretary did not
> believe that ELIZA … was a human.

> If the vertical-horizontal illusion is truly an illusion—and by the lights of
> Perceptual Error, it surely is—then it is one which I would suffer frequently
> in both Film and HMD. What I would resist, however, is …

Stacked hedges ("perhaps it might arguably be") do not occur.

### 2.6 Punctuation

| Mark | Rate per 1,000 words | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Parentheses | about 15 | Citations, asides, qualifications, jokes |
| Colon | about 5 | Introducing a definition, the content of a view, or a list |
| Semicolon | about 4 | Balancing two clauses of equal weight |
| Dash | 1 to 8, by publisher | Publisher house style; the LaTeX draft uses almost none |
| Question mark | under 1 | Framing a section's problem, then answering it |

The parenthesis is the characteristic mark. It carries the literature ("(de
Vignemont 2018; Metzinger 2003; O'Shaughnessy 1980/2008)"), the qualification
("(or at best shorthand)", "(perhaps in contrast to mental action)"), and the
joke ("(rightly!)", "(absurdly!)", "(a little too hot)", "(probably boring)",
"(ideally not our own)"). Where a dash might be used, a parenthesis or a pair
of commas is preferred.

Colons introduce what a name stands for: "the minimal view: given the nature
of the body, and representation of its parts, accounts … need not appeal to a
representation of the body as an integrated whole."

Single quotation marks are used to mention a term ('illusion', 'delusional
thinking', 'reasoning models'); double marks for direct quotation.

### 2.7 British forms and Latin

*Whilst* (variable; heavy in some papers, absent in others), *amongst*, *in so
far as* (three words), *judgement*, *favour*, *organise*, *behaviour*,
*sceptical*, *apposite*, *serviceable*. Latin tags are used sparingly and
without italics: *i.e.*, *e.g.*, *cf.*, *viz.*, *inter alia*, *a fortiori*,
*prima facie*, *tout court*, *modulo*, *qua*.

### 2.8 What is avoided

- **"X rather than Y" and "not X but Y".** By the author's own account these
  are to be avoided. The corpus bears this out: "rather than" runs at about
  0.7 per 1,000 words, most often inside a paraphrase of someone else or a
  technical description, and "not X but Y" at about 0.1. Contrast is made in
  other ways (see 3.3).
- **Slogans and fragments.** No sentence without a verb; no "Nobody needs to
  read the manual."
- **Punchy tricolons.** Lists of three occur, but as enumeration ("Charity,
  Coherence, and Rationalisation"), never as rhythm.
- **Intensifiers and brochure adjectives.** *Powerful*, *key*, *crucial*,
  *exciting*, *increasingly*, *shape* (as a verb of vague influence) do not
  appear. Where force is wanted it comes from a precise adjective with an
  edge: *lamentably common*, *woefully neglected*, *dizzyingly complex*,
  *inferentially overproductive*, *something of a scandal*.
- **The colon-list of three abstract nouns** ("interfaces, names, and
  metaphors that invite us to…"). The papers prefer one example, described.
- **Metaphors of labour** ("do the work", "draws these threads together",
  "unpack"). *Unpack* occurs once in 84,000 words.

## 3. Paragraph level

### 3.1 The opening move

The first sentence of a section or a paper is a plain statement or a concrete
case, and it is short. The abstraction comes second.

- "Try touching your nose, whilst touching your toes."
- "Sometimes what we perceive appears other than it is."
- "A generic conception of illusion has little value in theoretical discussions
  of perception."
- "Perceptual experience is perspectivally structured. Perceptual experience is
  self-locating. Failure to distinguish these claims masks a significant issue."
- "Scepticism can be a useful filter in eliminating dubious opinions."
- "My good friend Sally reaches for a mug of tea and takes a sip."
- "Birgitte, a clinician, incorporates an LLM service into her clinical
  reasoning over several months."

The case is always doing argumentative work, and it is homely: a mug of tea, a
chair one is thinking of buying, a childhood friend on television, Louis XIV at
lunchtime, a ventriloquist's dummy, a widower who accidentally forks a
conversation.

### 3.2 Claim, then reason

A claim is followed immediately by its ground, in the next sentence, often with
"For", "Because", or a colon. The reason is not left to the reader.

> But the model itself is not the kind of thing that can tell anyone anything,
> or indeed do anything. It is a complex mathematical entity conforming to a
> general structure, an architecture.

### 3.3 Concession, then turn

The most common paragraph shape is: grant the opposing point in its strongest
form; then "But"; then the correction, with its reason.

> Perhaps one could describe each of these as cases in which something merely
> has … 'an F-ish appearance', or as cases in which the objects in question are
> in fact F, such that they appear as they are. But I will characterize them in
> a manner that fits the generic description of illusion.

> In both cases, then, the person frame is not wholly misplaced. It makes
> salient a genuine structure of address … But it is inferentially
> overproductive.

This is also how contrast is handled without "X rather than Y". The two sides
are set out as separate sentences and then compared, using "By contrast",
"differ in", "whereas", or a flat denial: "I have argued that it does not";
"this is not so"; "The aim of this paper is to show why this is not so."

### 3.4 Signposting

Signposting is explicit and unembarrassed. Ordinal enumeration in parallel
sentences is a habit: "The first is a mistake about the kind of target. … The
second is a mistake about individuation. … The third is a mistake about
interpretation and continuity." Section plans are given in full ("In §ii, I
will argue … And in §iii, I will argue …"). On the website the section
numbers go, but "A second question is …" survives.

### 3.5 Landing the paragraph

Paragraphs end on a compact formulation of the point, frequently a chiasmus of
the form *took X for Y*:

- "She took properties of a kind for properties of a particular."
- "He took singularity of thought for singularity of continuation."
- "The Elizan takes the persistence of an interpretable target to sustain the
  persistence of a single interpretation."
- "These are not cases of illusion; they are cases of imaginative perception."
- "If Weizenbaum's secretary was mistaken, it is not obvious exactly how."

### 3.6 Handling the literature

Citations sit in parentheses and are generous. Disagreement is courteous, exact,
and occasionally barbed: "It is not my intention to pick on Smith. His is
simply a very clear and oft-cited example"; "Austin does not seem to have been
sufficiently persuasive"; "a manner of speaking that has become lamentably
common"; "The dearth of attempts to engage with either of these questions is
something of a scandal." Praise is warm and specific: "Strawson's wonderful
discussion", "the wonderful (but now deceased) journal Psyche".

### 3.7 Humour

Dry, brief, usually in parentheses or at the very end: "for all but the most
limber"; "Pardon me… what?"; "the necessary (probably boring) few pages"; "To
which, amen—modulo the unrestricted use of the term 'illusion'." Never a
set-up and punchline; never at a named person's expense.

## 4. Section and paper level

- **Openings** state the thesis in a sentence and the method in the next:
  "The aim of this paper is to show why this is not so. … My strategy (outlined
  in Sect. 3) will be to …"
- **Method** is the same across the corpus: distinguish two things that have
  been run together; name them; test the distinction against cases; say what
  falls out. The verbs of the method are *distinguish*, *prise apart*, *bring
  into relief*, *run together*, *lump together*, *fall out*.
- **Conclusions** restate what has been shown, mark what remains ("That
  challenge remains, to be taken up elsewhere"; "I leave that as a task for
  others"), and end on a formulation, sometimes a joke, never a call to action.

## 5. A working vocabulary

Words and phrases that recur and are safe to reach for: *apposite*, *posit*,
*construal* / *construe*, *in virtue of*, *notwithstanding*, *explanatorily
superfluous* / *potent*, *well-suited to*, *serviceable*, *muster*, *gesture
at*, *bring into relief*, *prise apart*, *run together*, *fall out*, *in the
grip of*, *a good deal of*, *a great deal of*, *blanket term*, *generic*,
*thoroughgoing*, *so-called*, *on this view*, *for present purposes*, *on
another occasion*, *there is a sense in which*, *it is not obvious exactly
how*, *warm up to the idea*.

## 6. Adjusting for the website

The site is read by colleagues, students, editors, and journalists in under
two minutes. So:

- **One paragraph per theme, 120–180 words.** Opening move, one named claim
  with its reason, one experimental or textual result, one link per claim.
- **Keep the first person and the opening case.** Drop the section plan.
- **One example per theme.** The papers use two or three; the site gets one.
- **Hedge once per paragraph at most.** "I take this to favour", "I suspect".
- **Citations become links**, in the same parenthetical position the papers
  would put them, labelled by venue and year.
- **No "rather than", no "not X but Y".** Set the two sides out and say which
  is favoured.
- **No brochure words.** If a sentence would sit happily on a product page,
  rewrite it.
- **Notes under publications and drafts** are two to four sentences: the
  case or the question first, then what the paper does with it. They are
  written as the abstract would be, in the first person where the paper is
  single-authored and in "we" where it is joint.

## 7. Checklist for new text

1. Does the first sentence state something plain, or give a case?
2. Is every view named, and is the name then used?
3. Does each claim have its reason in the next sentence?
4. Is there one "But" turn, with the concession before it made in good faith?
5. Is there at most one hedge, and does it fix scope?
6. Are the citations in parentheses, as links?
7. Is there any "rather than", "not X but Y", fragment, tricolon, or brochure
   adjective? Remove it.
8. Does the paragraph land on a formulation of the point?
9. Read it aloud. If it sounds like a grant abstract or a product page, start
   again from the case.
