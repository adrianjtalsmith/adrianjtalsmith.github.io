I am a Senior Lecturer in Philosophy at King's College London. Most of my work concerns bodily self-consciousness: how the body is represented, where we locate ourselves within it, and what it is to experience a body as one's own. A recurring theme is that a good deal of what seems simply given in experience is in fact the work of activity, bodily and mental, that goes unnoticed. That theme has led me more recently to the relation between perception and imagination, and to the ways in which people conceive of, talk about, and design generative artificial systems.

I joined King's in 2020. Before that I held research positions at the Jean Nicod Institute (École Normale Supérieure, Paris) and the Center for Subjectivity Research at the University of Copenhagen. I took my PhD at the University of Mainz, supervised by Thomas Metzinger, after degrees at Edinburgh and Warwick.

I am an Associate Editor of Analysis, and co-edit the Philosophy and Cognitive Science section of the MIT Open Encyclopedia of Cognitive Science. From 2021 to 2024 I was Philosophy Programme Chair of the European Society for Philosophy and Psychology.

Research

The bodily self

Try touching your nose whilst touching your toes. The parts you touch, and those with which you touch, are experienced as parts of a single integrated whole, and it is their integration that makes the task difficult for all but the most limber. On the standard view, explaining bodily experience and action of this kind requires a representation of the body as an integrated whole. I have argued that it does not: given the structure of the body itself, representations of its parts are enough (Synthese, 2021). A second question is where, within the body, one takes oneself to be. With Matthew Longo I have used pointing and perspective-taking tasks to show that self-location judgements distribute between the head and the torso, and that the balance between them can be shifted experimentally (2014, 2017). I take this to favour an account on which self-location depends on the subject's capacity for action, and to tell against accounts that derive it from the perspectival structure of perception alone (The Subject's Matter, 2017). A third question concerns ownership. I defend a cognitive account of the sense of ownership, on which one experiences a body as one's own only if one thinks of it as one's own, much of the relevant thinking going unnoticed (2015). In more recent work, including a paper on Locke (forthcoming in Locke Studies), I trace that thought to an affective awareness of the body as the locus of one's vulnerability. A book, Bodily Self-Consciousness, under contract with Routledge, develops these views together.

Perception, imagination, and illusion

Sometimes what we perceive appears other than it is. The term 'illusion' is often used to cover the whole variety of such cases, and I think little is gained by doing so. Some of them are better described as cases of imaginative perception, in which what we perceive appears as we imagine it to be: a friend appears as the character she plays; a virtual model appears as the chair one is thinking of buying (Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society, 2024). The imagining involved is not idle. It is structured by what Kendall Walton calls props, and it is often spontaneous and unnoticed, which is why it can be mistaken for something that perception does on its own. I have used this idea to explain reports of ownership in the rubber hand illusion (2015) and, with Matthew Longo, to ask what virtual reality can and cannot show about self-conception (2019). It applies, I suspect, just as well to the sense that there is someone one is talking to when one addresses a conversational machine.

How we conceive of artificial systems

Joseph Weizenbaum was startled to find that his secretary, who knew perfectly well that ELIZA was a program, asked to be left alone with it. Hundreds of millions of people are now in a structurally similar position. Many of them conceive of what they are interacting with as someone to be addressed, and if they are mistaken, it is not obvious exactly how. I call such people Elizans, and my current work asks what Elizan thought is about and where it goes wrong. The interfaces through which these systems are presented, the vocabulary of mind ('hallucination', 'reasoning', 'memory') that has migrated into talk about them, and the norms of conversation that users bring to them all invite one to think of a persisting individual on the model of a human person. That conception is not wholly misplaced. But it is inferentially overproductive: it encourages expectations of stable character, non-branching identity, and accountability that the technology can readily violate. Much of this work is joint with Alex Grzankowski and with colleagues in user-experience research at GraspingAI, and it turns in the end to design: what would an interface have to show its users for these mistakes to be avoidable?
