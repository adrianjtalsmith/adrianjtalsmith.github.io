I am a Senior Lecturer in Philosophy at King's College London. My research asks how our capacities for bodily and mental activity structure our experience of the world and of ourselves. Most of my published work is on bodily self-consciousness: how the body is represented, where we locate ourselves within it, and what it is to experience a body as one's own. More recently I have been writing about the relation between perception and imagination, and about how people conceive of, talk about, and design generative AI systems.

I joined King's in 2020. Before that I held research positions at the Jean Nicod Institute (École Normale Supérieure, Paris) and the Center for Subjectivity Research at the University of Copenhagen. I took my PhD at the University of Mainz, supervised by Thomas Metzinger, after degrees at Edinburgh and Warwick.

I am an Associate Editor of Analysis, and co-edit the Philosophy and Cognitive Science section of the MIT Open Encyclopedia of Cognitive Science. From 2021 to 2024 I was Philosophy Programme Chair of the European Society for Philosophy and Psychology.

Research

The bodily self

How does having a body of a particular structure shape what it is like to perceive and act? I have argued that we need not posit a representation of the body as an integrated whole in order to explain bodily experience and action: representations of body parts, together with the causal structure of the body itself, do the work (Synthese, 2021). With Matthew Longo I have used pointing and perspective-taking tasks to show that people locate themselves in both head and torso, in a pattern that can be shifted experimentally (2014, 2017). And I defend a cognitive account of the sense of body ownership, on which experiencing a body as one's own is a matter of thinking of it as one's own, prompted by an affective awareness of the body as the locus of one's vulnerability (2015; and, on Locke, forthcoming in Locke Studies). I am writing a book, Bodily Self-Consciousness, under contract with Routledge, that draws these threads together.

Perception, imagination, and illusion

Sometimes what we perceive appears other than it is. The blanket term "illusion" hides a variety of cases, and some of them are better described as imaginative perception: we perceive something while imagining it to be as it is not, prompted by a prop in Kendall Walton's sense (Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society, 2024). This gives a way of understanding body illusions, immersive virtual environments, and, increasingly, our interactions with conversational machines.

How we conceive of artificial systems

Generative AI systems are presented to us through interfaces, names, and metaphors that invite us to treat them as a "who" rather than an "it". With colleagues in philosophy and in user-experience research I am studying that invitation: the Elizan intuition that there is someone on the other side of the screen; the vocabulary of mind ("hallucination", "reasoning", "memory") that migrates into AI discourse and carries unintended commitments with it; and the norms that a conversational interface leads users to apply, which the underlying system is structurally unable to meet. Much of this work is developed with GraspingAI, a community of researchers, designers, and practitioners thinking about AI together; the current projects are listed here.

I also have long-standing interests in early modern philosophy, especially Locke on persons and bodies, and in the methodology of the cognitive sciences.
