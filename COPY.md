### Bio

I am a Senior Lecturer in Philosophy at [King's College London](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/people/adrian-alsmith). Most of my work concerns bodily self-consciousness: how the body is represented, where we locate ourselves within it, and what it is to experience a body as one's own. A recurring theme is that a good deal of what seems simply given in experience is in fact the work of activity, bodily and mental, that goes unnoticed. That theme has led me more recently to the relation between perception and imagination, and to the ways in which people conceive of, talk about, and design generative artificial systems.

I joined King's in 2020. Before that I held research positions at the Jean Nicod Institute (École Normale Supérieure, Paris) and the Center for Subjectivity Research at the University of Copenhagen. I took my PhD at the University of Mainz, supervised by Thomas Metzinger, after degrees at Edinburgh and Warwick.

I am an Associate Editor of *Analysis*, and co-edit the Philosophy and Cognitive Science section of the MIT Open Encyclopedia of Cognitive Science. From 2021 to 2024 I was Philosophy Programme Chair of the European Society for Philosophy and Psychology.

#### The bodily self

Try touching your nose whilst touching your toes. The parts you touch, and those with which you touch, are experienced as parts of a single integrated whole, and it is their integration that makes the task difficult for all but the most limber. On the standard view, explaining bodily experience and action of this kind requires a representation of the body as an integrated whole. I have argued that it does not: given the structure of the body itself, representations of its parts are enough ([*Synthese*, 2021](papers/alsmith-2021-bodily-structure-and-body-representation.pdf)). A second question is where, within the body, one takes oneself to be. With Matthew Longo I have used pointing and perspective-taking tasks to show that self-location judgements distribute between the head and the torso, and that the balance between them can be shifted experimentally ([2014](papers/alsmith-longo-2014-where-exactly-am-i.pdf), [2017](papers/alsmith-ferre-longo-2017-misalignment-paradigm.pdf)). I take this to favour an account on which self-location depends on the subject's capacity for action, and to tell against accounts that derive it from the perspectival structure of perception alone ([*The Subject's Matter*, 2017](papers/alsmith-2017-perspectival-structure-and-agentive-self-location.pdf)). A third question concerns ownership. I defend a cognitive account of the sense of ownership, on which one experiences a body as one's own only if one thinks of it as one's own, much of the relevant thinking going unnoticed ([2015](papers/alsmith-2015-mental-activity-and-the-sense-of-ownership.pdf)). In more recent work, including a paper on Locke ([forthcoming in *Locke Studies*](publications.html)), I trace that thought to an affective awareness of the body as the locus of one's vulnerability. A book, *Bodily Self-Consciousness*, under contract with Routledge, develops these views together.

#### Perception, imagination, and illusion

Sometimes what we perceive appears other than it is. The term 'illusion' is often used to cover the whole variety of such cases, and I think little is gained by doing so. Some of them are better described as cases of imaginative perception, in which what we perceive appears as we imagine it to be: a friend appears as the character she plays; a virtual model appears as the chair one is thinking of buying ([*Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, 2024](papers/alsmith-2024-unmistaken-imaginative-perception-and-illusion.pdf)). The imagining involved is not idle. It is structured by what Kendall Walton calls props, and it is often spontaneous and unnoticed, which is why it can be mistaken for something that perception does on its own. I have used this idea to explain reports of ownership in the rubber hand illusion ([2015](papers/alsmith-2015-mental-activity-and-the-sense-of-ownership.pdf)) and, with Matthew Longo, to ask what virtual reality can and cannot show about self-conception ([2019](papers/alsmith-longo-2019-using-vr-to-study-self-conception.pdf)). It applies, I suspect, just as well to the sense that there is someone one is talking to when one addresses a conversational machine.

#### How we conceive of artificial systems

Joseph Weizenbaum was startled to find that his secretary, who knew perfectly well that ELIZA was a program, asked to be left alone with it. Hundreds of millions of people are now in a structurally similar position. Many of them conceive of what they are interacting with as someone to be addressed, and if they are mistaken, it is not obvious exactly how. I call such people Elizans, and my current work asks what Elizan thought is about and where it goes wrong. The interfaces through which these systems are presented, the vocabulary of mind ('hallucination', 'reasoning', 'memory') that has migrated into talk about them, and the norms of conversation that users bring to them all invite one to think of a persisting individual on the model of a human person. That conception is not wholly misplaced. But it is inferentially overproductive: it encourages expectations of stable character, non-branching identity, and accountability that the technology can readily violate. Much of this work is joint with Alex Grzankowski and with colleagues in user-experience research at [GraspingAI](https://www.graspingai.com/), and it turns in the end to design: what would an interface have to show its users for these mistakes to be avoidable? The current projects are [listed here](work-in-progress.html).

### DRAFTS

**Elizan thought and generative artificial systems.** Draft, September 2026. [PDF]
> Note: Joseph Weizenbaum's secretary knew that ELIZA was a program and asked to be left alone with it all the same. If she was mistaken, it is not obvious exactly how. The paper takes her as the first of many Elizans: people who conceive of that with which they interact as someone to be addressed. Following a remark of Turing's, it fixes the target of Elizan thought, a conversational particular tracked through an exchange, in order to bring into relief the conception under which that target is regarded, and it works through the entities involved in an LLM service (model types, services, personas, instance and session agents) to ask which of them can bear that conception. Four kinds of mistake fall out: about the kind of target, about its individuation, about the continuity of its interpretation, and about whether its narration of its own conduct governs that conduct. The paper closes by asking what an interface would have to show its users for these mistakes to be avoidable.

### MINDS, METAPHORS, AND MACHINES

Section intro: A programme of work with Alex Grzankowski (King's) and colleagues in user-experience research. The common question is whether the concepts through which people understand generative artificial systems are adequate to the systems themselves, and what follows for design when they are not.

**Talk to me like a human.** With Aaron Kagan. GraspingAI, August 2026. [Read at GraspingAI](https://www.graspingai.com/thinking/talk-to-me-like-a-human/)
> Note: Conversation is a familiar way of interacting with a system; no one need read a manual. But nothing about interacting in language requires that the system be addressed as an interlocutor, and doing so has a cost: users apply norms of honesty, consistency, and commitment that the system is in no position to meet. The piece sets out this dilemma and some ways of designing around it.

**Working as intended: Narrow and wide conceptions of LLM failure.** Draft; presented at the Philosophy of AI Conference 2026, School of Advanced Study, London.
> Note: A model may work exactly as intended and yet fail to meet the norms of the situation in which it is deployed. The paper distinguishes narrow failures, internal to a model's operation, from wide failures of this second kind, and argues that some of what are called alignment problems are better described as cases of *normative incapacity*.

**Migrated metaphors: How AI vocabulary and interfaces shape thought.** With Alex Grzankowski. Draft.
> Note: Terms such as 'hallucination', 'memory', and 'reasoning' have a technical use within the field, but they acquire further commitments as they pass into public use. We offer a taxonomy of the families of metaphor now in circulation, an account of this migration, and a framework for judging when a metaphor is apt for a given purpose and audience. A short entry on AI metaphors appeared in the Philosophical Glossary of AI in May 2026.

**Generative interface design.** Design research with GraspingAI.
> Note: If the mistakes Elizans make are encouraged by the interface, then an interface can be designed to discourage them. This project develops a pattern library and a set of testable hypotheses for interfaces that do not imply a single, persistent interlocutor: they present several co-equal outputs, show sources before any synthesis of them, make memory inspectable and editable, and refer to the system in functional terms.

**Intentional Systems.** With Alex Grzankowski, Aaron Kagan, David Pearl, and Allen Bevans. Funded by a King's SPARK Innovation award, 2026–27.
> Note: A networked knowledge platform pairing short accounts of ideas from philosophy and psychology with their implications for the design of digital tools.

### BODILY SELF-CONSCIOUSNESS

**Bodily Self-Consciousness.** Monograph under contract with Routledge, New Problems in Philosophy series. Six chapters drafted.
> Note: There is an impersonal way of thinking about the body that one is: it is one body amongst others. There is also a first-personal way, grounded in perception, introspection, and affect. The book argues that bodily self-consciousness runs through a single hybrid concept of the body, reached by both routes. Along the way it argues that self-location depends on embodied agency and cannot be read off the perspectival structure of perception alone, that bodily awareness is a form of introspective perception, that the sense of ownership is a cognitive and affective achievement, and that no representation of the body as an integrated whole is needed to explain bodily experience.

**Body ownership and response-dependence.** Draft.

### COLLABORATION WITH GRASPINGAI

[GraspingAI](https://www.graspingai.com/), founded by Aaron Kagan and David Pearl, is a community of philosophers, designers, and user-experience researchers thinking about AI together; it describes itself as being for thoughtful people navigating the opportunities and implications of AI. My involvement grew out of a collaboration with Aaron on anthropomorphism, artificial selfhood, and interface design, which began during his research visit to King's in 2022. That collaboration informed design prototypes tested at Google and is now the basis of a research impact case study at King's. Through GraspingAI we publish accessible pieces drawn from work in progress, such as [Talk to Me Like a Human](https://www.graspingai.com/thinking/talk-to-me-like-a-human/), and develop tools such as [metaphorsin.ai](https://www.metaphorsin.ai/), a dashboard that maps the metaphors used for AI in public discourse.
